# American immigration law changes

# American Immigration Law: Recent Changes and Ongoing Shifts (2020–2024)

**Status Note:** *US immigration law is a highly volatile intersection of federal statutes, executive orders, judicial rulings, and agency regulations (primarily DHS, DOJ, and DOL). The following summary reflects the landscape as of early 2024. Many policies are subject to active litigation, meaning their enforceability can change rapidly based on court injunctions. Where specific dates or outcomes are contested, uncertainty is explicitly noted.*

---

## 1. Executive Branch Policy Shifts
The most significant changes in recent years have occurred via Executive Orders (EOs) and agency guidance rather than new Congressional legislation, leading to a "pendulum effect" between administrations.

### The Biden Administration (2021–Present)
*   **Reversal of "Remain in Mexico" (MPP):**
    *   **Action:** The administration sought to terminate the *Migrant Protection Protocols* (MPP), which required asylum seekers to wait in Mexico while their US cases were processed.
    *   **Status:** Terminated in October 2021. However, the policy was challenged in court.
    *   **Uncertainty:** As of early 2024, the Supreme Court has allowed the Biden administration to end the program, but legal challenges regarding the scope of the termination and the treatment of specific nationalities remain active in lower courts.
*   **Title 42 Termination:**
    *   **Action:** The public health order (Title 42) used to expel migrants at the border without processing was terminated on May 11, 2023.
    *   **Impact:** This returned border processing to standard immigration law (Title 8), theoretically allowing for asylum claims, though new restrictions were immediately layered on top.
*   **Asylum Bans (The "Circumvention of Lawful Pathways" Rule):**
    *   **Action:** In May 2023, DHS issued a final rule stating that individuals who cross the border illegally after entering the US from a third country (e.g., Mexico) are generally ineligible for asylum.
    *   **Mechanism:** Exceptions exist for those who applied for protection in a transit country or have a pending asylum application.
    *   **Uncertainty:** This rule is currently under intense judicial review. Several federal district courts have issued preliminary injunctions blocking parts of the rule, while the Supreme Court has declined to block it entirely. The final legal standing of this rule is **uncertain** and may vary by jurisdiction.
*   **Parole Programs:**
    *   **Action:** Expansion of "parole" authority (8 CFR 212.5) to allow specific nationals (e.g., Cubans, Haitians, Nicaraguans, Venezuelans - CHNV) to enter legally with a sponsor.
    *   **Constraint:** These programs are temporary and subject to the discretion of the DHS Secretary. They do not grant permanent status but allow for entry and work authorization.

### The Trump Administration Legacy (2017–2021)
*   **Remains in Effect (via Court Orders):** Several policies from this era remain in force due to court injunctions preventing the Biden administration from fully rescinding them.
    *   **Public Charge Rule:** The 2019 rule expanding the definition of "public charge" (making those likely to use benefits inadmissible) was vacated by the Supreme Court in 2022, but a new, narrower rule was finalized by Biden in 2024. The transition and specific application criteria are **uncertain** during the implementation phase.
    *   **Asylum "Transit Ban":** The 2019 rule barring asylum for those who did not seek protection in a transit country was vacated by the Supreme Court in 2021, but the legal principles are being re-litigated under the 2023 Biden rule mentioned above.

## 2. Legislative Landscape (Congress)
*   **Stalemate:** No major comprehensive immigration reform bills have passed Congress since the 1990s.
*   **Border Security Funding:**
    *   **Action:** In March 2024, Congress passed a bipartisan package (part of the Fiscal Year 2024 Omnibus) providing funding for border security, asylum processing, and humanitarian assistance.
    *   **Key Provisions:** Included funding for 1,000 new asylum officers and 1,000 new immigration judges.
    *   **Uncertainty:** The long-term efficacy of this funding in reducing backlogs is **uncertain** given the scale of the backlog (over 3 million cases) and the rate of new filings.
*   **State-Level Legislation:**
    *   **Trend:** States like Texas and Florida have passed laws attempting to enforce federal immigration law or restrict sanctuary cities.
    *   **Legal Status:** Most of these state laws are currently blocked by federal courts on the grounds that immigration enforcement is exclusively a federal power. The Supreme Court has not yet issued a definitive ruling on the constitutionality of these specific state enforcement mechanisms, leaving their future **highly uncertain**.

## 3. Judicial Rulings and Backlog
The judiciary has become the primary driver of immigration policy changes, often overriding executive actions.

*   **Supreme Court Precedents:**
    *   *Department of Homeland Security v. Regents of the University of California (2020):* Struck down the Trump administration's attempt to rescind DACA (Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals) due to procedural errors, but left the program's ultimate fate to Congress or a new administration.
    *   *Status of DACA:* As of early 2024, DACA remains in place but is under constant threat of being struck down entirely by the Supreme Court in future litigation. New applications are accepted, but the program is not a path to citizenship.
*   **The "Mandatory Detention" Loophole:**
    *   **Issue:** Courts have increasingly ruled that mandatory detention for asylum seekers violates due process if detention exceeds a reasonable time (often 6 months) without a bond hearing.
    *   **Current Practice:** DHS has attempted to detain more individuals, but courts frequently order releases. The balance between detention and release is **uncertain** and varies by circuit court.

## 4. Specific Visa and Employment Changes
*   **H-1B and Specialty Occupations:**
    *   **Action:** The Biden administration has attempted to reverse Trump-era restrictions on H-1B visas (e.g., the "prevailing wage" levels).
    *   **Uncertainty:** The Department of Labor (DOL) finalized a rule in 2024 to raise prevailing wage levels, which will likely increase costs for employers. The impact on visa approval rates is **uncertain** but expected to be restrictive for lower-wage tech roles.
*   **Green Card Backlogs:**
    *   **Status:** Family-based and employment-based green card backlogs have grown significantly.
    *   **Policy:** The "Immigration Act of 1990" caps remain in effect. No legislative changes have been made to increase these caps, leading to multi-year waits for applicants from high-demand countries (e.g., India, China, Mexico, Philippines).
*   **Student Visas (F-1):**
    *   **Action:** Increased scrutiny on international students, particularly regarding "remote learning" restrictions post-pandemic.
    *   **Current Rule:** Students can now study remotely from abroad under certain conditions, but the specific requirements for maintaining status are complex and subject to frequent updates by USCIS.

## 5. Areas of High Uncertainty and Risk
*   **The 2024 Election Cycle:**
    *   **Risk:** Immigration is a central campaign issue. A change in the presidency in January 2025 could result in the immediate reversal of current Biden-era policies (e.g., reinstatement of MPP, new asylum bans, or changes to DACA).
    *   **Note:** Executive actions can be reversed quickly, but statutory changes (laws passed by Congress) are harder to undo.
*   **Litigation Outcomes:**
    *   Many current policies (especially the 2023 Asylum Ban) are operating under "shadow" status, where they are technically in effect but blocked in specific regions by federal judges. A final Supreme Court ruling could either nationalize the ban or strike it down entirely.
*   **Enforcement Discretion:**
    *   The definition of "priorities" for ICE (Immigration and Customs Enforcement) changes with every administration. Currently, the focus is on national security and recent border crossers, but this is a policy choice, not a law, and can shift overnight.

## 6. Summary of Key Takeaways
*   **No New Statutes:** The core framework of US immigration law (INA) remains largely unchanged since 1996 (IIRIRA).
*   **Executive Dominance:** Policy is currently driven by DHS/DOJ regulations and Executive Orders, making it unstable and subject to rapid reversal.
*   **Judicial Check:** Federal courts are actively blocking or modifying many executive actions, creating a patchwork of enforcement.
